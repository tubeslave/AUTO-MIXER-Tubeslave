- **Discovered/read:** 2026-10-11
- **Reading depth:** full_text_complete, pages 1–8
- **Version status:** accepted-manuscript/conference lineage; do not count the IEEE record as independent confirmation

### Method and results actually read

The paper fits a single-input/single-output frequency response with a pole–residue model. It uses sub-band PolyMAX/LSCF, stabilisation diagrams across model orders, clustering of stable poles, and least-squares residue recovery. Time-domain resynthesis uses independent damped second-order oscillators and a stable low-dispersion finite-difference update.

The authors first test 100 synthetic modes between 20 and 520 Hz. Ninety-five stable poles are explicitly identified; the five missing modes lie near identified components. Reported synthetic-case frequency errors are generally below 0.1%, while decay-rate and mode-shape deviations are typically within 2%.

For measured RIRs, one source–receiver pair from each of three historical halls is analysed from 20 Hz to 12 kHz. The measurements used ISO 3382-1 layouts, a dodecahedron plus subwoofer, half-inch free-field microphones, 512k-sample ESS at 48 kHz, and commercial acquisition software. The three selected fits used 9,072, 11,475 and 52,470 modes. Modal reverberation times in 500–1000 Hz were close to conventional estimates. The authors report real-time synthesis of up to 20,000 modes under 15% of one M2 CPU core.

### Limitations

- The method fits and resynthesises individual RIRs; it does not itself define or validate interpolation across positions.
- Measured cases use one highlighted source–receiver pair per hall; no held-out positions or cross-room generalisation protocol is reported.
- The paper shows residual plots but gives no aggregate reconstruction-error table, confidence intervals or listener test for measured RIRs.
- Spurious poorly stabilised modes cause post-response ringing. Tighter decay thresholds trade identification efficiency for fewer artifacts; smoothing decay times reduces ringing but introduces an early-decay phase shift.
- The reported CPU result is hardware/implementation specific and is not evidence of perceptual quality.
- This source shares authors and project lineage with the Bunkervik demo and is not independent confirmation of that implementation.

### Limited conclusion

Sub-band modal analysis is a plausible and computationally efficient representation for large measured RIRs. It supplies the modal components later used by the Bunkervik demo, but it does not validate common-basis clustering, moving-position rendering or usefulness on a rock mix.

## Source Card SRC-ML-20261011-1800-02

**Auralization based on multi-perspective ambisonic room impulse responses** — Kaspar Müller and Franz Zotter, Acta Acustica 4 (2020), article 25.

- **Stable IDs:** doi:10.1051/aacus/2020024; arxiv:2312.02581
- **Journal record:** https://doi.org/10.1051/aacus/2020024
- **Open full text:** https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.02581
- **Published:** 2020-11-18; later arXiv deposit is the same version family, not a second source
- **Discovered/read:** 2026-10-11
- **Reading depth:** full_text_complete, pages 1–18
- **Licence:** CC BY 4.0

### Method and listening evidence actually read

The method starts with spatially distributed first-order Ambisonic RIRs. A local triplet is decomposed so direct sound and selected early reflections can be matched to common sound events, extrapolated to the desired listener position, and only then distance-weighted and interpolated. Residual RIR content is handled separately. The paper also describes a real-time path based on an offline-refined grid, direct-sound time alignment and spectral correction.

The listening experiment used a measured 30-position IEM CUBE grid with 2 m spacing and a simulated room grid. Offline interpolation produced a 0.2 m fifth-order grid. Stimuli were reproduced with head-tracked DT-770 Pro headphones in an anechoic room, randomized in a MUSHRA-like interface. Nineteen experienced listeners participated; one unreliable response set was excluded. Speech probed localization and pink noise probed coloration.

Plain distance-weighted linear interpolation was rated significantly below all correspondence-aware variants for static localization and coloration (p < 0.001). For static coloration, matching direct sound plus ten early reflections outperformed direct sound alone (p < 0.001). In dynamic trials, the full method approached the simulated reference for smoothness of coloration; direct-sound/early-event correspondence and residual extrapolation mattered more for coloration than localization.

### Limits and transfer boundary

- This is independent of the NEMUS/Bunkervik author group, but it tests Ambisonic early-event geometry, not modal-pole clustering.
- The experiment used one measured room, one simulated room, speech and pink noise—not a multitrack rock production.
- The paper-specific 2 m coarse grid, 0.2 m refined grid, 100 ms late boundary, 1024-sample update and eleven matched peaks are implementation/test values, not production presets.
- Participants rated localization and coloration, not drum attack, groove, mono fold-down, commercial-mix preference or automixer quality.
- The authors disclosed patent priority rights; the paper licence does not itself grant an implementation licence.

### Limited conclusion

Independent listening evidence supports the general proposition that direct/event correspondence and time alignment can reduce coloration and abrupt spatial changes relative to naive linear RIR interpolation. It does not validate the Bunkervik modal algorithm or a generic moving-reverb effect.

## Knowledge Cards

### KC-ML-20261011-1800-01 — RIR synthesis is not spatial interpolation

A method that accurately fits and resynthesises each measured RIR separately does not establish that parameters from different positions can be interpolated coherently. Correspondence between positions is a separate construct and validation problem.

- **Confidence:** high
- **Applies to:** modal and event-based measured-room systems

### KC-ML-20261011-1800-02 — correspondence evidence transfers only at principle level

The independent Acta study supports aligning direct sound and prominent early events before interpolation. It does not independently validate Bunkervik’s common-pole clustering, modal residues, 6,300-pole model or “Aura” control.

- **Confidence:** medium-high
- **Citation independence:** independent authors and implementation; adjacent task, not direct replication

### KC-ML-20261011-1800-03 — reconstruction and CPU metrics are not listener evidence

Pole-fit error, residual spectra, modal count and CPU load establish representation and deployment properties. They do not establish coloration, localization, mix preference, attack preservation or mono/stereo safety.

- **Confidence:** high

### KC-ML-20261011-1800-04 — paper implementation values are not presets

Grid spacing, matched-event count, update size, late boundary and stabilisation thresholds depend on measurement geometry, room, representation and renderer. None is transferable to a rock mix without a local preregistered test.

- **Confidence:** high

## Revisions and contradiction handling

The 15:00 rule and experiment were too broad. A static-versus-moving test in an arbitrary processor would probe a creative modulation preference, not the source claim about correspondence across measured positions. That design is withdrawn and replaced by an alignment-only RIR interpolation test.

The independent Acta result does not contradict Bunkervik; it sharpens the evidence boundary. Both point toward correspondence/alignment, but they use different representations and endpoints. They are not duplicate versions and are not counted as direct replication.
