## Sources actually read

### `SRC-ML-20260927-0900-01` — adaptive room equalization

- **Title:** *A DDSP Framework for Adaptive Room Equalization*
- **Primary:** <https://dafx.de/paper-archive/2026/papers/DAFx26_paper_48.pdf>
- **Stable ID:** `DAFx-26:paper_48`
- **Publication date:** `2026`
- **Discovery date:** `2026-09-27`
- **Depth:** full proceedings PDF, including framework, estimator/controller methods, experiments, ablations, compute table, limitations and conclusion

The paper places differentiable signal processing inside a closed-loop playback calibration system. A linear equalisable IIR path is updated from an online estimate of the room transfer function. This is a room/playback problem; it is not evidence that a creative track EQ should chase a learned or measured spectral target.

Evaluation combines measured SoundCam room impulse responses with ten MedleyDB full mixes at 48 kHz. Processing uses non-overlapping 8,192-sample frames—about 170 ms, or roughly six updates per second—and an exponentially smoothed response estimate with 5% new estimate and 95% history. These are settings of one controlled experiment, not validated live-sound or mastering presets.

The target is derived from the initial room impulse response rather than an independently specified artistic response or a multi-position target. This anchors the controller to one initial transfer condition. A practical implementation must define whose response is being optimized, protect other listening positions and stop safely when the estimator lacks reliable excitation.

Runtime was measured on an RTX 5090 and excludes audio I/O and operating-system scheduling jitter. First-order optimization took about 20 ms per frame, Newton and iHAM3 about 140 ms, and FxLMS about 201 ms, which exceeds the nominal 170-ms frame. These measurements do not establish real-time operation on the project's target computer or console path.

The stated limitations are material: the study is a controlled linear simulation built from measured RIRs, not a physical closed loop. It excludes low-SNR crowd noise, loudspeaker and microphone nonlinearities, converter quantization, thermal drift and uncontrolled listener motion. The appropriate conclusion is that the framework is a promising calibration research path requiring estimator-confidence gates, an independent target and hardware validation—not a deployable automatic EQ rule.

### `SRC-ML-20260927-0900-02` — time-varying all-pass clipping guard

- **Title:** *A Clipping Prevention Method for All-Pass Digital Filters with Time-Varying Coefficients*
- **Primary:** <https://dafx.de/paper-archive/2026/papers/DAFx26_paper_45.pdf>
- **Stable ID:** `DAFx-26:paper_45`
- **Publication date:** `2026`
- **Discovery date:** `2026-09-27`
- **Depth:** full proceedings PDF, including derivation, first- and second-order cases, experiments, discussion and limitations

The method computes a safe coefficient increment for time-varying first- and second-order all-pass filters when the input is already bounded by a declared sample maximum. In the paper's stress case, a 100-Hz sine at amplitude `0.99` is processed at 48 kHz while a coefficient moves rapidly between approximately `-0.99` and `+0.99` over about 1 ms. An additional music excerpt demonstrates the same mechanism.

The guard is not transparent by construction: when an intended update would violate the sample bound, the applied coefficient follows another path and catches up later. That changes the phase/group-delay trajectory and may change transients or stereo relationships even when no discrete output sample clips.

The proof is tied to Direct Form I output equations. The paper explicitly notes that other realizations may clip internally. It constrains discrete output samples, not intersample/reconstructed true peak. For the second-order case, splitting the available prevention budget equally between the two coefficients is a practical heuristic rather than an established optimum.

Evidence is limited to controlled case studies and contains no listening test, broad programme corpus, plugin integration or target-hardware result. The paper supports a topology-specific candidate safety mechanism, not a general limiter, declipper or permission to automate all-pass coefficients aggressively.

The paper is marked CC-BY-4.0, but the repository has no root licence. A licence exists only for one vendored dependency. Bundled SoundCam RIRs and MedleyDB mixes have no per-file licence/provenance manifest. Paper, code and audio rights must therefore be checked separately; the bundled audio is not a legal Reference Card or multitrack source.

The audio loader discovers files, downmixes to mono, resamples and peak-normalizes inputs. More importantly, `save_wav()` independently peak-normalizes every saved input, desired, no-EQ and EQ render. The metrics evaluator then reloads those files to compute RMSE, SI-SDR, LUFS difference and related measures. Independent normalization removes absolute gain relationships, invalidates the meaning of LUFS-difference as a controller outcome and can change waveform-metric rankings. Raw gain-preserving float outputs must be the metric source; loudness-matched copies belong only in a separate blind-listening path.

Packaged provenance is internally inconsistent: `results/main_experiment/config.json` declares `max_num_songs: 2`, while `results/main_experiment/metrics.csv` reports `n_files=10` for each group. The config and table cannot be treated as one reproduced run until a manifest and hashes reconcile them. Optional metric dependencies are also absent from the environment file; the shipped table records missing MRSTFT and spectral-centroid values.

The target builder derives the target from the initial RIR and includes a hard-coded factor of one half. That factor needs documentation and a sensitivity test before any comparison is interpreted as the effect of the adaptive optimizer.
