## Full-text source review

### SRC-ML-20261007-1500-01 — JASA accessibility mixing transforms (upgraded)

- title: *Exploring level- and spectrum-based music mixing transforms for hearing-impaired listeners*
- authors: Aravindan Joseph Benjamin; Kai Siedenburg
- publication: *Journal of the Acoustical Society of America* 154(2), 1048–1061; published `2023-08-22`
- DOI: `10.1121/10.0020269`; PMID: `37607002`
- official metadata: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37607002/
- full text read: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/373295416_Exploring_level-_and_spectrum-based_music_mixing_transforms_for_hearing-impaired_listeners
- licence shown in article: `CC BY 4.0`
- reading depth: `full_text_methods_results_discussion_limitations`
- independence: primary study; the 2025 follow-up below is the same-author research lineage

Methods: experiment 1 used 25 normal-hearing listeners and 20 hearing-impaired listeners split equally between hearing-aid users and non-users. Ages and hearing loss were heavily confounded: the normal-hearing group averaged 28 years, while the two hearing-impaired groups averaged 70 and 67 years; mean hearing loss was 42 dB for hearing-aid users and 29 dB for non-users. Participants adjusted one ungraduated control at a time over 8-second MedleyDB excerpts played at 80 dBA: lead-to-accompaniment ratio (LAR), a full-mix spectral slope from 125 Hz to 8 kHz around a 1 kHz pivot, or a per-track EQ-transform. The transform linearly extrapolated each track's spectrum from a level-normalized ensemble reference (0%) through the factory spectrum (100%), smoothed the residual spectrum and broadband-normalized the processed track before summing.

Results: in experiment 1, mean LAR was `-0.92 dB` for normal-hearing, `+1.68 dB` for hearing-aid users and `+1.32 dB` for non-users. None of the group means was a statistically robust deviation from zero, but the mixed-effects model found a group effect and hearing-aid users differed from normal-hearing listeners (`p=.008`, `d=1.06`). Spectral-balance group differences were not significant; the non-user subgroup alone preferred `+0.6 dB/oct` relative to zero (`p=.04`). The EQ-transform had no group effect; only normal-hearing participants significantly preferred a mean below the factory value.

Experiment 2 tested 18 hearing-aid users within subject, with and without aids, using counterbalanced phases and 60 MedleyDB/Cambridge-MT tracks. Without aids versus with aids, average LAR was `13 dB` versus `8 dB` (`p=.028`, `d=.6`), spectral balance `+0.64` versus `-0.74 dB/oct` (`p=.002`, `d=.9`) and EQ-transform `156%` versus `74%` (`p=.03`, `d=.5`). These are condition-specific group means, not presets.

Limitations: experiment 1 confounded hearing-loss severity with hearing-aid use and age; the study elicited preference rather than task performance; effects were tested separately; hearing-aid insertion gain/input-output behaviour and loudness-growth functions were not characterized; hearing-aid saturation was not directly controlled; backing vocals were omitted from LAR; excerpts were short; and the EQ reference/factory spectra are corpus- and mix-dependent. The authors describe the work as a first exploration.

Bounded conclusion: expose LAR/spectral controls only for an explicitly defined accessibility target and let the intended listener adjust them under the actual hearing-aid state. The data do not justify automatic vocal priority, a fixed `+2 dB` setting, or a general spectral-sparsity target for rock mixes.

### SRC-ML-20261007-1800-01 — PLOS ONE performance follow-up

- title: *Effects of spectral manipulations of music mixes on musical scene analysis abilities of hearing-impaired listeners*
- authors: Aravindan Joseph Benjamin; Kai Siedenburg
- publication: *PLOS ONE* 20(1), e0316442; published `2025-01-10`
- DOI: `10.1371/journal.pone.0316442`
- full text: https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0316442
- data: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.14541533
- licence: `CC BY 4.0`
- reading depth: `full_text_methods_results_discussion_limitations`
- independence: same authors and transform lineage as the JASA study; stronger follow-up, not an independent vote

Methods: 30 young normal-hearing and 24 older hearing-impaired listeners completed 200 target-present/absent trials using 2-second MedleyDB excerpts, five-track mixes and fixed target-to-mix level ratio of `-10 dB`. Targets were Lead vocals, Bass, Drums, Guitar and Piano. Hearing-aid users removed their aids. EQ-transform levels were `0, 100, 200, 300%`; the same level was applied to target and mix tracks. The design used a generalized linear mixed-effects model with participant and trial random intercepts.

Results: hearing loss and target class affected detection, but EQ-transform had no main effect (`p=.07` overall; `p>.2` within the two groups). Median accuracy was `84.9%` for young normal-hearing and `79.6%` for older hearing-impaired listeners. Lead vocals were easiest (`100%` and `92.5%`); Bass was worst (`67.5%` and `60%`). For older hearing-impaired listeners, Bass detection decreased monotonically as transform strength rose (`p=.03`), and neither group improved with over-mixing above 100%. A higher mix Gini index and a larger target-minus-mix 95%-roll-off difference correlated with performance (`r=.3`), but the transform levels were applied to different tracks and did not significantly order Gini sparsity (`p>.18`), so those correlations do not establish a causal transform benefit.

Limitations: age and hearing loss were confounded, musical training differed between groups, samples were small/unbalanced, pure-tone audiometry did not characterize auditory-processing disorder, aids were removed, stimuli were two seconds, and transform levels were not applied to identical track sets for the objective comparison.

Bounded conclusion: subjective preference and target-detection performance are separate endpoints. Spectral sparsity is a diagnostic descriptor, not an automatic objective. Target-class-specific evaluation is mandatory; Bass is an explicit rollback gate.

## Knowledge Cards

### KC-ML-20261007-1800-01 — accessibility mix controls must be listener-specific

Population means from short preference tasks are not production presets. Record intended listener, hearing profile, hearing-aid state and playback context, then retain per-listener settings only if repeated choices are stable. Confidence: `medium`; transfer outside the studied population: `low`.

### KC-ML-20261007-1800-02 — preference and detection are distinct endpoints

The JASA preference study and PLOS ONE detection follow-up address different outcomes. A listener may prefer a transform that does not improve target detection, and a descriptor correlation does not prove that the transform caused the benefit. Confidence: `high` for endpoint separation.

### KC-ML-20261007-1800-03 — target class is a rollback dimension

Lead-vocal targets were easiest and Bass targets hardest in the follow-up; stronger transformation monotonically worsened Bass detection for older hearing-impaired listeners. Any accessibility-processing test must report results per target class and reject aggregate wins that hide Bass regressions. Confidence: `medium` for this task; generalization to full songs: `low_to_medium`.

### KC-ML-20261007-1800-04 — same-lineage follow-up is not independent confirmation

The 2025 PLOS ONE study directly challenges a performance extrapolation from the 2023 preference paper, but both share authors and the EQ-transform lineage. Treat it as a stronger within-program correction, not an independent vote. Confidence: `high`.
